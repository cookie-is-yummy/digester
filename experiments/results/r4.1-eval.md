## Method

- **Scoring.**
  - All 18 run files were scored on dev (44 searches: 22 `ig`, 22 `yt`). The controls cover only their own scope's 22 searches.
  - Each search's metrics are averaged over the 3 rewrite repeats.
  - CIs are 2,000-sample bootstraps over searches. Comparisons pair repeat k with repeat k, average each search's Δ over the repeats, then bootstrap. W/L/T uses |Δ| > 0.005.
  - Per-kind rows count a search under every kind it carries, so they overlap.
  - Recall is standard (hits / |relevant|). YouTube searches often have hundreds of answers, so R@10 is small by construction.
- **Pooling.**
  - Seed-only unjudged@10 was 0.45–0.60 overall for the combined runs (Instagram scope 0.38–0.85).
  - Every unjudged dev top-10 item across all 18 files was pooled: 1,066 items. That includes cross-platform items: 292 YouTube items in Instagram searches and 265 Instagram items in YouTube searches.
  - After the merge, unjudged@10 is 0.00 for every run.
- **Blind judging.**
  - The evaluator judged every item; no LLM judge was used.
  - The sheet was grouped by search and shuffled within each search, with no run identity or rank shown.
  - Each item was judged against the search's meaning: the intent where one exists, otherwise the query. For more-like-this searches, the example items stood in.
  - Text shown:
    - Instagram: the caption, hashtags, creator, collections and interaction dates;
    - YouTube: the title, channel, playlists, watch, like and save dates and counts, and the duration;
    - the video-pass description, only for untitled YouTube items (17 items had no own text).
  - te-v1c text was not shown, as in round 1. This slightly disfavours C1 and C2, which surface thin items.
  - Cross-platform items were graded by meaning, not platform. An Instagram reel can answer a YouTube search. Short-form items lost a grade where the intent asks for long videos.
  - Backup before the merge: `evalset.pre_r4.1.jsonl`.
- **Share judged relevant, by scope and the item's platform:**
  - Instagram searches: 35% of Instagram items, 40% of YouTube items;
  - YouTube searches: 26% of Instagram items, 8% of YouTube items.
  - The YouTube–YouTube cell is low because round 3 had already judged those searches' top 10s; only stragglers were left.
- **Consistency caveat.**
  - Instagram labels are now mostly this evaluator's: the round-0 seeds plus 638 new judgments.
  - YouTube own-platform labels are mostly round 3's.
  - Cross-platform judgments for YouTube searches come from a different judge than the YouTube items they compete with. A small difference in strictness between the two judges would move the YouTube interference numbers.

**Reading.**
- The combined index does **not** flood either platform with noise.
- On Instagram searches it swaps the weaker half of the Instagram top 10 for YouTube items that are almost as often relevant. Several topic and how-to searches have good YouTube answers. The relevant count is unchanged.
- On YouTube searches, two Instagram items per top 10 are relevant a bit over a third of the time.
- The cost shows up in **first-hit rank**: MRR falls by 0.04–0.05 on both scopes.
  - By kind, Instagram loses on name (−0.07), said_shown (−0.11) and vibe (−0.13).

  - YouTube: 0.487, +0.011 [−0.002, +0.025] vs `c0yt`.
  - So C0's own-platform ordering is slightly worse than the control's on Instagram. The likely cause is BM25 idf and length normalisation fitted to a corpus that is 84% YouTube.
  - The cross-platform items then make up that loss.
- **Verdict.** No balance fix is urgent for C0. Per-platform BM25 normalisation might recover about 0.02 on Instagram first-hit rank, but that is small.

## C1: te-v1c lists everywhere, and the coverage confound

**Why C1 hurts YouTube.**
- te rows exist for 98% of Instagram items but only 6% of YouTube items, so most Instagram items get four RRF votes and most YouTube items two.
- On YouTube searches, Instagram items rise from 2.05 to **5.30** of the top 10 (pooled labels: 53% of slots). Their precision is 0.35, below the YouTube items they displace (0.56–0.59).
- On Instagram searches, the reverse: YouTube items fall from 4.56 to 1.20 per top 10.

**How much of C1's YouTube loss is crowding?** Each ranking was projected to its own platform:

| Projection | Score | vs control | vs C0 projection |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1, yt items only | 0.448 | −0.028 [−0.118, +0.062], 9/11 | −0.039 (C0 projection 0.487) |
| C1, ig items only | 0.564 | +0.051 [0.000, +0.107], 15/6 | +0.076 (C0 projection 0.488) |

**Decomposition of C1's YouTube loss.**
- Within YouTube, the te lists cost about −0.03 to −0.04. That is close to round 3's −0.046 for A1 vs A0, so the effect reproduces.
- The remaining ≈ −0.095 of the gap between C1's actual YouTube score (0.353) and its YouTube-only projection (0.448) is the coverage confound: Instagram items with extra votes crowding the top 10.
- On Instagram, the te pass is worth about +0.05 to +0.08 in either view. That matches round 1's +0.059, now with OpenAI vectors.

**By kind.**
- **Helped on Instagram:** said_shown +0.32, people +0.24, time +0.14, place, more-like-this and topic.
- **Hurt on YouTube:** topic −0.22, task −0.19, structure −0.17, more-like-this −0.19. These are the precise-topic kinds round 3 saw hurt.
- Vibe and time gain on YouTube, as in round 3.

**Verdict.**
- C1 fails overall.
- Its Instagram gain is real but a near miss (CI touches 0), and it costs the enrichment pass ($0.18 per 1k items).
- A fair test needs fusion that doesn't hand extra votes to whichever items happen to have te rows (agenda 1).

## C2: routing te by information sufficiency

The design fails because of how it was built:
- The te BM25 and te dense lists were restricted to the 8,250 insufficient items.
- RRF is rank-based, so each list's top ranks go to thin items **whatever their similarity**. Every insufficient item then competes with two extra full votes.
- Pooled top-10 mix:

| | ig, C0 | ig, C2 | yt, C0 | yt, C2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Insufficient items per top 10 | 2.03 (all cross-platform) | **7.98** at P 0.31 | 3.45 at P 0.48 | **8.50** at P 0.30 |
| Sufficient items per top 10 | 7.97 at P 0.72 | 2.02 at P 0.85 | 6.55 at P 0.58 | 1.50 at P 0.72 |

- On Instagram, C1 had 0.67 insufficient items per top 10; C2 has 7.98.
- The C2 − C1 Instagram result (−0.318, 0/21/1) therefore says nothing about whether thin captions benefit from enrichment. It says that a separate list restricted to a subset is a strong prior for that subset.
- On YouTube, C2 equals C1 (−0.000). The thin-item flood there replaces C1's Instagram flood.

**Test the cascade again with routing at the item level, not the list level** (agenda 2): add an insufficient item's te text to its own view, or let te lists vote only above a score threshold.

## Problems with the runs (none invalidates a result)

1. **C2's routing is implemented as list membership.** That creates a strong thin-item prior (see above). It matches the preregistered wording ("te rows rankable only for insufficient items"), so C2 is a fair negative result for *this* design only.
2. **C1's comparison is confounded by enrichment coverage** (98% of Instagram items vs 6% of YouTube items). The projection gives the de-confounded estimates.
3. **"Watched" is YouTube-only.** Any C3 `require watched` intent therefore filters out Instagram. It is a hidden platform filter, which BRIEF-4 asks to use only when the user names the platform.
4. **The qi-v3 rewrite script also sent the 13 test searches to gpt-5-nano.** The builder disclosed this; they appear in no run. They were not scored here.
5. **Judge mix.** Instagram labels are now mostly this evaluator's. The cross-platform labels for YouTube searches come from a different judge than the YouTube items they compete with (see Method).
